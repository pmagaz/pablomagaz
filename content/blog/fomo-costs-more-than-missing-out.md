The new phone comes out in September. The one in my pocket works perfectly well, but the new one has a better camera, the reviews are everywhere, and two people at work already have it. By Friday it feels less like a purchase and more like something I'm falling behind on.

That feeling has a name, the fear of missing out, and it's behind more bad decisions than most of us would admit. It also has a very precise meaning in investing, and it's worth a minute to explain, because the same pattern shows up with phones, consoles, frameworks and holidays.

## What the fear of missing out actually costs

FOMO, the fear of missing out, is the anxious feeling that everyone else is getting something we aren't. In investing it has a very concrete shape. A company's shares have been rising for weeks. The news covers it, friends mention it, and the people who bought early are visibly making money. Someone who had never looked at it before buys in, not because they think it's worth the price, but because it has already gone up and they can't bear to watch it go higher without them.

Very often they're buying right at the top, from those early buyers, who are happy to sell. Then the excitement fades and the price falls back. They paid the highest price precisely because so many others felt the same thing at the same moment.

Take a new video game console at launch. Full price, sometimes more from a reseller. A handful of games, half of them unfinished. A first batch of hardware with the problems nobody has found yet. A year later it costs less, the library is three times bigger, the issues are fixed, and the games we bought it for are on sale.

The people who queued on launch day didn't get a better console. They got the same one, earlier, for more money, with less to do on it. What they actually paid for was not having to wait.

That's the shape of it. By the time something is everywhere, on every feed and in every conversation, most of the easy value has already been taken by the people who were early for a reason. What's left is the highest price and the most competition. Joining at that point is buying at the top.

And the thing we're afraid to miss almost never disappears. There's another phone next September. There's another console generation, another sale, another flight. Missing out on this one costs very little. Chasing it costs the premium, every time.

> Missing out is usually cheap. Chasing is what's expensive.

## Professionally: the framework everyone is moving to

I've been writing software long enough to have watched this one several times over. A new framework or tool shows up, the conference talks are about nothing else, and within months there's a quiet worry that staying on the old one means our skills are expiring. Some teams rewrite. Some engineers spend every evening learning it.

A few of those tools become the standard. Most don't. The ones that do are still there two years later, better documented, with the rough edges sanded off and the answers on every forum. Learning them then is cheaper, and nothing important was lost by waiting. The ones that fade take every hour spent on them along.

None of this means never learning anything new. It means learning things for a reason we can say out loud, not because everyone else is.

## At corporate level: doing it because the competitor did

Companies have the same reflex with a bigger budget. A competitor announces something, and by the next week there's a project to do the same. A new technology becomes the topic of the year, and every roadmap suddenly has a line for it, whether or not anyone knows what problem it solves.

The cost isn't only the money. It's the work that gets pushed aside to make room, usually the less exciting work that was going to matter more. And a company that copies every move ends up always one step behind, paying the most for things its customers didn't ask for.

The better question is the one nobody wants to ask in that meeting: if nobody else were doing this, would we? If the honest answer is no, being second or fifth costs much less than it feels like it will.

## Personally: where the pressure is loudest

Social media is a machine built to produce this feeling. The trip everyone seems to be taking this summer. The restaurant with the queue round the block. The trainers that sold out in an hour. The investment a friend made a fortune on, mentioned exactly once, at the peak.

Most of it follows the same pattern as the console. By the time it reaches us, it's at its most crowded and most expensive. The restaurant will still be there when the queue is gone. The place will still be beautiful outside peak season, at half the price.

The rule I try to keep is simple. When I notice I want something mainly because other people have it, I wait. A week for small things, a month for big ones. If I still want it after the noise has moved on, I buy it, usually for less. Most of the time I don't, and I'm glad I waited. It hasn't made me the first to have anything. It's made the things I do have the ones I actually wanted.
