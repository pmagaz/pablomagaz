Most of us have a story like this. Something starts going wrong, just a little. We stay, because it will turn around. It gets worse, and now walking away would mean admitting a real loss, so we stay a bit longer. The small loss we refused at the start becomes the big one we're forced to take at the end. I have that story more than once, and each version was paid for in full.

What it eventually taught me is that the loss was never the problem. Refusing to accept it was.

There's a simple fix, and it comes from trading, where it's called a stop loss: a point, decided before we start, where we get out no matter what. Ignore where it comes from. It works without knowing anything about markets.

## What accepting a loss actually means

First, what a stop loss is, for anyone who has never bought a share. When people invest in something, say shares in a company, the price moves up and down every day. A stop loss is an instruction given in advance: if the price falls to a certain level, sell automatically. Someone buys at 100 and sets the stop at 90. If the price drops to 90, they're out, with a loss of 10. Not pleasant, but known and limited. Without it, the same person watches 90 become 80, then 60, telling themselves at every step that it will come back.

The stop loss exists because, at 90, nobody wants to sell. So the decision is made earlier, by someone who isn't losing anything yet.

A loss is just information: something we chose isn't working. Accepting it early keeps it small. The trouble is how each option feels. Holding on feels like hope. Getting out feels like failure. So we hold.

Take the bus that doesn't come. After ten minutes, walking would get us there in fifteen. But we've already waited ten, so we wait five more. Now we've invested fifteen, and leaving feels even more wasteful. Twenty-five minutes later the bus arrives, and we tell ourselves waiting was the right call.

Or the old car. Every repair is going to be the last one. Each bill on its own is smaller than a new car, so each one gets paid. Added up, they bought the new car a few times over.

Both work the same way. We look back at what we've already put in, instead of forward at what staying will cost. And the longer we stay, the more there is to look back at, so the harder leaving gets.

That's the whole point of deciding the exit in advance. Before we start, nothing is at stake yet. Later, the person who has to make the call has pride, money and hope tied up in it, and that person is very bad at this decision.

> A small loss accepted today is the cheapest version of that loss we will ever be offered.

## Professionally: the cost of one more year

The job that stopped working two years ago. One more year for the bonus. One more year because the promotion is close. Nothing goes wrong on any single day, which is why it never feels like a loss. It's only visible looking back, as the years that could have gone somewhere else.

Then the decision we defended in a meeting. The architecture, the hire, the tool we pushed for. Admitting it was wrong costs some face, once. Not admitting it costs the team months, and keeps costing. In my experience, the people who say "I was wrong, let's change it" early aren't remembered as wrong. They're remembered as the ones who could be trusted to say so.

## At corporate level: the projects nobody dares to kill

Every company has one. The six-month project in its fourteenth month. Nobody kills it, because killing it means someone signed off on a mistake. So it gets more people, a new deadline and a fresh slide deck.

The cost of cancelling a project is easy to see, it's what we already spent. The cost of keeping a zombie project alive is on no report at all: the people, the attention and the better ideas it keeps waiting.

The fix is boring and works. Write down the kill criteria at kick-off, when nobody owns the project emotionally yet. "If by March we don't have this many users, we stop." Then, when March comes, nobody has to be the one who gives up. The decision was already made by people who had nothing to defend. The best teams I've worked with treated an early kill as a win, because it was.

## Personally: where letting go is hardest

The degree chosen at eighteen. The friendship that only goes one way. The investment we're waiting on to "get back to even." That phrase is the tell. Life doesn't know what we paid for something, and doesn't owe it back. Getting back to what we had is a feeling, not a plan.

The better question is simple and uncomfortable: if I were starting today, would I choose this? If the answer is no, what we've already put in doesn't change it. It's gone either way. The only thing still up for decision is how much more goes with it.

Accepting a loss doesn't mean pretending it didn't hurt. It means letting it be the size it actually is, and not letting it grow. Then letting it go, which is the second half and the one people forget. A loss carried into the next decision, as the need to win it back, tends to produce another one.

So before anything that matters, I decide what would make me walk away, and I write it down. When it shows up, I don't renegotiate with myself. It hasn't made me lose less often. It's kept my losses small enough to recover from, and that's the only reason I've been able to keep going.
