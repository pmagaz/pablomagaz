Most of us are trying to be right. After many years of building engineering teams, and  trading accounts that have charged me real money for the lesson, I am fairly sure that is the wrong target. What decides how things end up is rarely how often you were right. It is the risk reward ratio you accepted to get there.

The term comes from trading. Ignore that part. You do not need to know anything about markets to use it.

## What the risk reward ratio actually is

The risk reward ratio is one plain question: how much can I lose here, compared to how much I can win? Lose ten to win thirty and the prize is three times the price. The other way round, it is not. 

Say you apply for a job you suspect you are not ready for. It costs you an afternoon and a polite no. If it lands, the next three years of your life look different. That is a very good risk reward ratio, because you are aiming to win far more than you can lose, and it means you can be turned down nine times out of ten and still finish well ahead.

Now the other direction. You answer a message at the wheel and win ten seconds. The best thing that can happen to you is almost nothing. The worst is that there is no next time. And you can be right hundreds of times in a row, which is exactly why it keeps feeling reasonable while you are doing it.

Two ordinary decisions, and what separates them isn't how likely you are to be right. You're far more likely to get home fine than to get the job. It's what each one pays you if it works, against what it costs you if it doesn't.

That gap is the risk reward ratio, and most of the time nobody names it. The course you keep thinking about, the message you haven't sent, the ten minutes you try to make up on the motorway. Each one has an answer to the same two questions: what do I get in the best case, and what does this take from me in the worst. Most days are full of small versions. It's worth building the habit on those, because the big ones don't arrive labelled.

> Being right is not the goal. Winning more when you are right than you lose when you are wrong is the goal.

## Professionally: the risk reward ratio on your own desk

Ask for the raise. The cost is one uncomfortable conversation. The win is the money, and every salary after it that gets negotiated up from this one. Ask five times, hear no four, and you're still miles ahead of never having asked. It still gets put off for months, because the discomfort is today and the win is abstract and somewhere later. The discomfort is real. It just isn't the risk.

The position nobody warns you about is the comfortable job where nothing bad ever happens. The cost never shows up on a day you could point to, it's skills ageing quietly while the field moves on. The win is capped at whatever this year's pay rise turns out to be. You're risking a lot for very little, and every single day of it feels fine.

## At corporate level: every roadmap is a set of bets

The bad ones share a shape. The eighteen-month replatform. The big-bang migration. The two-year programme with all the value parked at the end. Huge cost if it goes wrong, decent gain if it goes right, and it has to go right nearly every time. It gets approved in a room where everyone understands the technology and nobody has asked what happens if it doesn't land.

Most good engineering practice is the same question in working clothes. Ship it to a handful of users first. Run it as a pilot. Keep releases small, do iterations and build things so they can be undone. None of that makes the prize bigger. It makes being wrong cheap, which is worth just as much and is far more under your control.

## Personally: where it stops being theory

Exercise, sleep, putting a bit aside each month. Small, boring, repeated costs against a payoff a long way off. Skipping one on any given day costs you nothing you'd notice, which is exactly why it's so easy. Over ten years it's the best deal available to almost anybody.

Then the rule that beats every other line in this post: never take a bet you can't come back from. Not a bad outcome. A final one. The risk reward ratio only means anything if you're still there for the next decision, so protect that first and worry about the rest afterwards.

What I actually do is ask two questions before anything that matters. What do I get if this works? What does it take from me if it doesn't? If the second answer is everything, I walk away, whatever the first one promises. That's the whole method. You don't need to be right more often. You need being right to be worth more than being wrong costs you, and once the risk reward ratio points that way, you can afford to be wrong all week and still come out ahead.
