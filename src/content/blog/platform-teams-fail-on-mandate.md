Every company I have joined in the last decade had already tried to build a platform team. Most of them had the right engineers and the wrong agreement.

## The Pattern That Repeats

A group is carved out of product engineering, told to "reduce friction", and measured on nothing in particular. Six months later it is a ticket queue with a roadmap nobody outside the team has read.

The post-mortem usually blames the technology: wrong abstraction, wrong timing, too much Kubernetes. It is almost never the technology. The constraint was the mandate, and there wasn't one.

## A Mandate Is Three Sentences

A working mandate names three things: the decisions the team owns, the decisions it explicitly does not own, and the outcome it is accountable for.

If you cannot write that in three sentences a product director will repeat back to you unprompted, you do not have a mandate yet. You have a team name.

> If the platform team can be overruled by any product manager with a deadline, it is not a platform team. It is a favour.

The second sentence — what the team does *not* own — is the one people skip, and it is the one that does the work. A boundary that only says "yes" is not a boundary.

## Who Has to Write It

Here is the uncomfortable part: the mandate is not the platform team's to write.

It belongs to the executive who owns delivery, which is usually the CTO. Asking the team to define its own authority is asking it to grant itself something it cannot grant. Delegating that authorship is the most common failure I see in this pattern, and the cheapest one to fix.

## What to Do on Monday

Three concrete steps, in order:

1. Pick the three interfaces the platform will own outright for the next two quarters. Not ten. Three.
2. Publish them where product teams will actually read them.
3. Defend the first product team that gets told no — publicly, and in the same week it happens.

That third step is the whole thing. Nothing communicates a mandate faster than a decision that holds under pressure, and nothing dissolves one faster than the first exception granted quietly.
